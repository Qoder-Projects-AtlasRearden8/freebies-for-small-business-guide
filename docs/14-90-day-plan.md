# The 90-day plan: one small session a week

**The job:** Turn this guide into a working business system without losing a weekend. One 45–60 minute session per week, one real outcome per session. If a week goes sideways, you have not failed; you have a plan with a next step.

> **This is education, not legal, tax, or financial advice.** The plan organizes free tools and learning. It does not decide your entity, taxes, contracts, insurance, or licenses. Book a free SBDC or SCORE session early, and use a licensed professional for decisions that matter.

## The rules

1. **One bottleneck at a time.** Do not sign up for every tool in week one.
2. **Use real work.** A real invoice, a real contact, a real page. Practice data teaches nothing.
3. **Stop at "working," not "perfect."** You can polish after the business has a pulse.
4. **Export before you depend.** Test the exit door while the tool is still free.
5. **If you miss a week, skip don't stack.** Do the next session; don't do two in one sitting and resent the plan.

**A realistic time budget:** 45–60 minutes for the session, plus a few minutes during the week to gather information. Week 5 may take two sessions if you are learning hosting from scratch. That is normal.

## What each session actually produces

**Weeks 1–4: the foundation.**
By the end of week 4 you should be able to answer: Where does business email live? Where does money go? How do I ask for payment? Who am I following up with, and when? If those answers are clear, the rest is easier.

**Weeks 5–8: the front door.**
You do not need a perfect brand. You need a page that explains what you do, a way to contact you, a way to book you, and a way to stay in touch with permission. Publish before you feel ready, then fix what confuses real people.

**Weeks 9–12: the back office.**
This is where free tools earn their keep: simple signatures, free advising, one useful skill, and a backup you have actually restored. A backup you have never tested is a hope, not a backup.

**Week 13: the review.**
Open every tool's pricing page. Compare your usage to the limit. Cancel what you don't use. Decide whether one paid tool would save more time than it costs—and write down why. "No" is a valid answer.

## The maintenance rhythm after 90 days

| Rhythm | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | 15 min | Relationship review: next actions, waiting-on, one useful follow-up |
| Monthly | 25 min | Money date: reconcile, categorize, check invoices, export CSV and P&L |
| Quarterly | 30 min | Export all data, check free-plan limits, review AI privacy, update links and prices |
| Annually | 60 min | Review tools, insurance, licenses, tax questions, and whether the business still fits you |

Put these on the calendar with the same seriousness as a client meeting. The business is also a client.

## The low-energy version

Some weeks you will have 15 minutes and a flat battery. Do this instead of skipping entirely:

- **Tiny week:** open the tool, do one transaction, write one next action, or send one follow-up.
- **Admin-only week:** download one export and put it in the backup folder.
- **No-buy week:** cancel one unused subscription or decline one upsell.
- **Ask-for-help week:** send one short email to the SBDC, SCORE, or a mentor.
- **Rest week:** read one chapter and write one question. That counts.

ADHD-friendly, low-energy productivity is not a moral failure. It is a design requirement. Smaller sessions are allowed.

## If you only do three things

1. Separate the money and keep a monthly money date.
2. Put a one-page website and a way to contact you online.
3. Book free human help before you pay for advice.

Everything else can wait until a real limit appears.

```text

[ ] Pick one bottleneck
[ ] Set a 45-minute timer
[ ] Open only the tools for this session
[ ] Do the real task, not the tutorial
[ ] Export or save the result
[ ] Write one next action with a date
[ ] Close the laptop

DONE LOOKS LIKE
What I can now do without looking it up:

What still blocks me:

Who I will ask:

When my next session is:
```
