# E-signature: get simple documents signed without paper

**The job:** Send a document for signature, keep a record of who signed and when, and save the completed copy where you can find it. Electronic signatures are ordinary business practice, but the tool is not a lawyer.

> **This chapter is not legal advice.** Whether an electronic signature is enforceable, what notices are required, and which documents can be signed electronically depend on the document, the parties, and the law. Federal ESIGN and Indiana's Uniform Electronic Transactions Act generally recognize electronic signatures, but there are important exceptions and practical proof requirements. Ask a licensed attorney for anything consequential. Never use a generic tool for wills, court filings, or documents where a professional has told you not to.

## The free starting point: DocuSeal

[DocuSeal](https://www.docuseal.com/pricing) is an open-source e-signature platform. Its hosted free plan is small—currently **10 emailed documents per month**, with unlimited templates—and it also offers a self-hosted option via the open-source project. For a one-person business that signs a few simple client agreements a month, the free plan can be genuinely useful.

Why it's a good fit:

- Simple templates and signing fields.
- An audit trail and completed PDF.
- Open-source code available for self-hosting if you have the technical ability.
- No need to pay for basic signing on day one.

Why self-hosting is not "free" for everyone:

- You need to run and secure a server.
- You own patches, backups, uptime, and access control.
- Misconfigured self-hosted software can expose private contracts.
- Email deliverability and SMS verification may require extra services.
- Self-hosting is a technical project, not a beginner requirement.

If you don't need an online workflow yet, a PDF emailed with clear instructions and a returned signed copy can work for some low-risk documents. That doesn't remove the need for an appropriate agreement or proof; ask a professional when signatures must meet a specific standard.

## What an e-signature is—and isn't

An electronic signature is a way to show intent to sign a record electronically. It is not just a typed name in a contact form. A useful workflow records:

- The document that was presented
- The signer's identity/email and intent
- The time and date
- Consent to do business electronically, where appropriate
- The final signed document plus an audit certificate
- A copy provided to each signer
- A retention plan for your business records

The tool helps with the mechanics. **The agreement and the circumstances determine enforceability.** This guide can't tell you whether a particular document qualifies. For significant agreements, use an attorney.

## Set up a small signing workflow

1. **Decide if this document should be signed online.** Wills, certain family/court documents, and some notices may be excluded or need specific formalities. Ask a lawyer if you're unsure.
2. **Use a document you have the right to use.** Don't upload someone else's contract without permission.
3. **Create a template only for repeatable, low-risk documents.** Examples: a simple client agreement your attorney has reviewed, a speaking release, or a basic author-services schedule.
4. **Add fields deliberately.** Signature, date, initials, name, and maybe an agreed checkbox. Don't ask for extra sensitive information.
5. **Set signing order.** Who signs first? Does the other party sign after you?
6. **Write a plain-language email.** Identify the document, why it's being sent, and who to ask with questions. Don't pressure anyone to sign immediately.
7. **Test with your own email.** Sign a dummy document. Check the final PDF, audit trail, and confirmation messages.
8. **Save the completed document.** Put it in a private folder with a date and descriptive name. Back it up. DocuSeal is not your permanent records system.
9. **Give each signer a copy.** Keep the final PDF and any certificate. Don't rely solely on a dashboard link.
10. **Delete or archive drafts according to your retention plan.** If a document contains personal information, limit who can access it.

## Signature caution checklist

- Is this document consequential enough that a professional should draft or review it? If yes, stop.
- Are all pages present, legible, and the correct version?
- Do the names, dates, addresses, and payment terms match reality?
- Is each signer authorized to sign for their side?
- Is the signature placement correct?
- Does the signer receive a completed copy?
- Does the audit trail identify the signer and timestamp?
- Have you stored it privately and backed it up?
- Have you avoided collecting ID numbers or health data that you don't need?
- Would you be comfortable explaining the process in a dispute? If not, get advice.

## Free-plan watch-outs

- **10 documents/month is a real ceiling.** Count envelopes, not pages. Check what DocuSeal counts before sending a batch.
- **Branding and advanced features may be limited.** That's okay for a small operation.
- **Self-hosting transfers responsibility to you.** Include server, backup, security, and maintenance costs in the decision.
- **Free tools do not provide legal review.** A signature does not make a bad agreement good.
- **Completed signatures contain personal data.** Don't put them in public GitHub repos, unencrypted shared drives, or AI chats.
- **SMS verification may cost extra or be unavailable.** Confirm the workflow you need.

Explain this contract excerpt in plain English, sentence by sentence:
